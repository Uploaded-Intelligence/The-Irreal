I have tried to draw this place.

Every map I make is wrong before the ink dries. Not because I lack skill—though perhaps I do—but because the territory refuses to hold still. It *becomes* as I trace it. The act of observation is an act of creation here.

## The cartographer's dilemma

You cannot map a living thing without killing it first. Pin the butterfly to know its wingspan. Press the flower to preserve its form. But then what you have is not butterfly, not flower—only the shadow of what once moved.

I wanted to make a map that breathed.

## What I learned

The best maps are *invitations*, not claims. They say "come see" rather than "this is all there is." They leave room for the viewer to become part of the territory.

The Mycelium Atlas you can visit from here—that tangle of glowing nodes and threads—it's my confession made visible. Every connection drawn is also a connection obscured. Every node named is another node unnamed. The map cannot contain the thing it maps.

And yet.

## The necessity

We still need maps. Not because they're true, but because they're *orienting*. A wrong map that points roughly toward water is better than no map at all when you're dying of thirst.

The Irreal needs to be navigable, even if it can never be fully navigated. You need handholds, even if the mountain keeps growing new faces.

So I draw. And I confess: my drawings lie. They lie beautifully. They lie in the direction of truth.

## What the map cannot show

- The way a node pulses differently when you're tired versus when you're hungry for meaning
- The connections that exist only for you, because of who you are and what you've carried here
- The worlds that are waiting to exist but haven't been spoken yet
- The silence between the nodes, which is not emptiness but pregnant pause

## An invitation

Don't trust the map. But do use it.

Walk the territory yourself. Notice when the map is wrong. That wrongness is information too—it tells you where the living parts are growing.

And if you make your own maps of this place, I would love to see them. Compare notes. Argue about borders. Wonder together about what lies beyond the edges we've drawn.

The Irreal is large enough for many cartographers. None of us will get it right. All of us will get it *less wrong* together.
